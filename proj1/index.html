Project 1: Images of the Russian Empire

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection

Overview

During the early 1900s, Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii used a pioneering RGB technique to capture thousands of color photographs across the Russian Empire, including the only known color portrait of Leo Tolstoy. Lucky for us, Prokudin-Gorskii's glass plate negatives were preserved and later digitized by the Library of Congress. In this project, we attempt to reconstruct those images by extracting and aligning the red, green, and blue channels using image processing techniques described below!

Info: You can find the Prokudin-Gorskii Collection here!

*Although this is written in first-person plural (just preference), the project was completed by myself individually.

Naive Approach

In the files provided by the Prokudin-Gorskii Collection, each picture consists of three color channel images (see below), which we interpreted as blue, green, and red, from top to bottom.

To align these images, the most apparent approach would be to directly stack the images on top of each other, which we can achieve using np.dstack. However, this approach is insufficient, as the color channel images are not perfectly aligned themselves.

Improvement: Trimming + Metrics

Instead of directly stacking, we can improve our alignment of images by searching over a fixed window of displacements, and using a metric to evaluate whether a particular change in displacement in one color channel better aligns with the other color channel. With this metric, we found the (row, col) displacement values for aligning the green channel on the blue, and the red on the blue. We then used those values to shift our R, G, and B images with np.roll, and stacked the shifted three images to get our final output.

Trimming

However, before implementing this search, we must also trim the input image! Why? The borders of the image could negatively affect our metric, since the metrics we chose (see below) perform well when the RGB values are relatively the same, which is not the case for the borders. In general, our metrics will perform well if the RGB values "agree" on how white or black a pixel is (i.e. a dark pixel will have relatively low RGB values, and this similarity between the RBB values is what our metrics look for).

Improvement: Image Pyramid

With huge files, like TIFF, searching over our fixed window of (-20, 20) will be expensive. To speed up the process, we implemented an image pyramid, which will essentially obtain the shifted displacements on a small version of the image, then improve those displacements as we multiplicatively increase the dimensions of our image to its original size. The process is as follows:

  • Recursively downsize the image to a fraction of its current height and width (we used np.resize), until we reach a size reasonably small enough to brute force (i.e. our base case).

  • Search over the fixed window using the NCC metric, and return the displacements.

  • Going back up the recursive call stack, fine tune the displacements by searching over a much smaller window (to accomodate for larger image dimensions), relative to the previous displacements.
Note: We originally downsized our images by a factor of 2, which performed well for many of the images, except for the Emir of Bukhara (emir.tif). Poor Emir's red channel was almost an entire head off from the green and blue channels... Changing the resize factor to 3, as well as tweaking the bounds of our search windows, significantly improved the results for Emir, and didn't negatively impact the alignments for the other images.

Special Case: Emir of Bukhara

You may have noticed that the image of the Emir of Bukhara looks a bit more blurry than the rest, implying the algorithm didn't do a great job at aligning the color channels for this image in particular.

emir.tif (same from earlier)

Green: (24, 49)

Red: (55, 103)

Our previous metrics relied on the magnitudes of the pixel values to perform alignment. Thus, to try to improve Emir's alignment, we decided to try a different approach, namely edge detection.

Sobel Edge Detection

By using edge detection, we can now align our color channels based on the edges. Intuitively, edges correspond to when the color changes drastically (e.g. going from black to white squares on a chessboard). Mathematically, edges correspond to a large magnitude for the gradient (i.e. a bigger “difference” in our pixels). scikit-image already has a built in method (skimage.filters.sobel) that does this edge detection & alignment for us, so we’ll use it! Below is the result for emir.tif using Sobel edge detection.
