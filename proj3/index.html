Warping Images

Earlier, how did we apply our homography matrix to our image? We cannot simply apply the homography to every pixel in our original image, then map it to the location in our warped image. For some intuition as to why, image if our warped image's dimensions were much bigger. Then, not every pixel in our warped image will be filled, if we mapped every input pixel to it.

Instead of forward warping, we'll use inverse warping! That way, we won't have unfilled holes in our warped image, since we're reverse mapping from the warped pixel to the original pixel(s). First, we'll find the inverse of our $H$ matrix, to find where every warped pixel "lands" in our original image. Then, we'll interpolate the value based on the nearby landed pixels (since our "landing" spot isn't always a perfect integer coordinate). We'll consider two methods for interpolation:

  • Nearest Neighbor Interpolation: we round our "landing" (x, y) to the nearest integer, then use that pixel's value.

    - Pros: NN is faster, since we only consider one value per pixel (no averaging). NN also doesn't hallucinate, meaning that all colors shown are colors that are present in the original image.

    - Cons: NN can result sharp edge artifacts, such as unwanted edges when interpolating a curve.

  • Bilinear Interpolation: we'll find the four pixels neighboring our "landing" position, then take their weighted average depending on their x and y distances from the "landing" position.

    - Pros: Bilinear gives better/smoother transitions, i.e. no unwanted sharp artifacts.

    - Cons: Bilinear is slower because of the averaging. Because it has smoother results, it may have worser results if sharp edges are desired.

Mosaicing

After warping an image, how can we stitch it together to another?

As you may have noticed earlier, even if we warp one of our images to match the other, the two images still aren't aligned yet! In other words, we need to figure out how to overlay them such that they form one complete picture, a mosaic.

Step 1: Padding our Images

First, we need to pad our images (specifically in the x and y directions) such that they have the same dimensions. The padding should be done such that:

  • the minimum and maximum x and y coordinates from both images are captured, and
  • the (x, y) and (u, v) points line up when the two images are stacked together.

Step 2: Masking our Images

If we directly add our two images together, we'll see that the pixels in the overlap region will stand out significantly, since they are roughly doubled in value.

Naive Adding:

To adjust these overlapped values, we can apply masks to the images in the overlapping region. For the overlapping region, we considered a binary alpha mask and a linear alpha mask.

Binary alpha mask: for every pixel in the overlap region, we use 0.5 of the value in one image, then 0.5 of the value in the other image.

Linear alpha mask (columnwise): for every pixel in the overlapping region, we'll use a greater proportion of the value in the closer image, and a lesser proportion of the value in the further image. For our images, "distance" was determined using the x coordinate (columns).

Linear alpha mask (Euclidean distance): for every pixel in the overlapping region, we'll use a greater proportion of the value in the closer image, and a lesser proportion of the value in the further image. For our images, "distance" was calcuated as Euclidean distance. To do so:

  • First, we create a mask for our individual image, where the value of the mask pixel ([0, 1]) depends on its distance from the four edges of the image. This mask determines the alpha channel for our image, so pixels closer to the edges of the image will have a lower alpha.

  • Then, we warp our alpha-adjusted image to the proper shape (depending on our homograpy H; see example below). Now, using the alpha values of our properly padded images, we compute a mask for the padded version of our image, using the formula alpha = dtrans1 / (dtrans1+dtrans2), where dtrans are the alpha values of our individual image. Below is the example of our final mask with the boba images.
  • Finally, We apply the final mask to our padded images, then ceil all the alpha values to 1, so our final image is solid.

Part 2: Feature Matching for Autostitching

Earlier, we computed our homography by manually selecting the correspondences. In this part, we'll see how we can do it automatically!

Harris Corner Detection & ANMS

Using Harris corner detection, we can automatically find "corners"/points using their gradients in the x and y directions. However, Harris corner detection itself gives us many, many points:

We want to reduce the number of points, only keeping the most "obvious" corners. However, we also want the points to be relatively spread out (so they aren't all in one place). To do this, we implemented Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression as described in this paper. Now, our selected points (200 in this example) are nicely spread out!

Seems like some parts of this fridge needs cleaning...

Feature Descriptor Extraction

Now that we have our corners, we want to obtain our correspondences, i.e. pairs of points across the two images. To find our correspondences, we need to first "describe" each of our points using a feature descriptor, then match points that have similar enough descriptions.

For each point, we calculated the feature descriptor by:

  • 1) Obtaining a 40x40 pixel patch around the point

  • 2) Downsampling the 40x40 patch to our extract 8x8 feature descriptor

  • 3) Normalizing the extracted feature descriptor such that the mean is 0 and the standard deviation is 1 (to account for different brightnesses).

Feature Matching

We've obtained our points from ANMS, and we've obtained our feature descriptors for each of those points. The next step is to match points with similar enough feature descriptors.

For feature matching, we used Lowe's technique, as described in the same paper mentioned above. We implemented feature matching as follows. For each point in image 1:

  • Find the two points in image 2 with feature descriptors closest to itself (determined by Euclidean distance), aka the two nearest neighbors.

  • Take the ratio of the distances: $$\frac{NN1_{dist}}{NN2_{dist}}$$

  • If the ratio is below a certain threshold, we include our point (from image 1) and its $NN1$ (from image 2) as a found pair.

We'll add a last step of using RANSAC to filter out poor pairings to get our final correspondences!

RANSAC

RANSAC, RANdom SAmple Consensus, is an algorithm we use to remove outliers from our pairings after feature matching, giving us (with high probability) the set of points from which we can accurately compute our homography. Given our initial set of pairings and a number of iterations, we perform RANSAC numIter times, each time doing the following:

  • 1) Randomly sample 4 points (without replacement), and construct a homography matrix from those points.

  • 2) For that homography matrix, count the number of inliers ($(H * im2\_pts) - im1\_pts \leq threshold$).

  • 3) After all iterations are done, return the homography matrix with the highest number of inliers (and optionally the inliers for that matrix).
